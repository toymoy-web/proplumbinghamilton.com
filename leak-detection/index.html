Hidden leaks in walls, floors and ceilings · Hamilton, Ohio

Emergency Leak Detection in Hamilton, OH

Leak detection in Hamilton, OH is finding where water is escaping when you cannot see it: inside a wall, above a ceiling, behind a shower or under a floor. The goal is to open one small, exact spot instead of tearing out a room to search.

Most people who call only know that water is going somewhere it should not. Finding where is the whole point of this service. Call (513) 796-9381.

Common signs of a hidden leak

  • A bill that rose while water use stayed the same.
  • The meter moves with every fixture off.
  • A stain, bubble or soft spot on a wall or ceiling.
  • A hiss or trickle in the walls with every tap closed.

First, confirm there is a leak

Turn off every faucet, toilet and appliance, write down the meter reading, and wait 30 minutes without using water. If it moved, water is going somewhere. Then close the main shutoff in the basement and check again.

If the meter stops once the main is closed, the leak is inside the house, which is what this page covers. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the buried service line, covered on our water line repair page. A running toilet flapper is the most common invisible leak of all, so rule it out first with a little food dye added to the tank.

Emergency service, day or night.

When a hidden leak becomes an emergency

Most leak detection can wait for a booked visit. It becomes an emergency when water is coming through a ceiling, a wall is soaking from the inside, or the meter races even with the main nearly closed. Every hour after that adds drywall, insulation and flooring to the bill.

Close the main shutoff first, then call. With the water off, the person on the line can tell you whether the house is fine until morning or whether someone should head out tonight with acoustic and thermal equipment.

Where leaks hide in Hamilton houses

In older homes in German Village, Dayton Lane and Rossville, the usual suspects are corroded galvanized fittings, old shower valves behind plaster, and drain connections under clawfoot or built-in tubs. Plaster holds moisture differently than drywall, so stains can appear some distance from the actual leak.

In newer homes, leaks are more often at PEX crimp fittings, supply lines to toilets and faucets, and dishwasher or ice maker connections. If the leak turns out to be under a concrete floor rather than in a wall, our slab leak page explains that separate process.

What to do before we arrive

  • Shut off the main if water is actively dripping or spreading.
  • Photograph stains and any damaged ceiling or wall before touching them.
  • Do not poke holes in a bulging ceiling unless water is ready to pour through, then put a bucket under it first.
  • Keep electricity off in any room where water is near light fixtures or outlets.

Water bill doubled or the meter keeps running

The most common reason people call for leak detection is a bill that jumped, sometimes tripling in one cycle, or a utility notice saying the meter turns while nothing is in use. The meter test on this page tells you whether water is leaving the system. It does not tell you where.

The split that matters is the buried line versus the inside of the house. Closing the main and watching the meter answers that. Leaks on the buried side follow the water line process, and leaks inside follow the methods above.

A stain on the ceiling under a bathroom

A stain on a dining room or kitchen ceiling under an upstairs bathroom is one of the most frequent leak calls. The source is usually the shower valve, the tub drain or overflow, a wax ring under the toilet, or the supply line to a vanity.

Running each fixture one at a time while someone watches the ceiling narrows it down. Water that only appears during a shower points to the drain or valve. A drip with nothing running points to a pressurized supply line, which needs attention first.

Finding the leak is priced apart from fixing it

Many callers want the leak found and marked, then plan to repair it themselves or hand it to a contractor. That works. Detection is quoted as its own step, the location is marked, and the repair is a separate decision with its own price.

If another plumber already came out and could not find it, say so on the call. Knowing what was ruled out saves time on the search.

Irrigation, hot tub and outbuilding lines

Outdoor leaks show up as a soggy patch, a small swamp in the yard, or a meter that only races while the irrigation valve is open. The usual sources are irrigation supply lines, hot tub plumbing and lines to a garage or barn. Closing that branch and rechecking the meter isolates them quickly.

A remodeled spa that started leaking afterward usually has a fitting or drain connection that was disturbed during the work. Irrigation systems also need a working backflow assembly, covered on our backflow testing page.

What leak detection costs depends on

Finding the leak and fixing it are priced as separate steps, so you know what each costs.

  • How many methods are needed to pinpoint the source.
  • Size of the area being searched.
  • Access through finished ceilings, tile or cabinets.
  • The repair itself, quoted once the leak is found.
  • Timing, since active leaks after hours cost more.

Many policies cover damage from a sudden hidden leak and sometimes the cost of accessing it, but not gradual leaks or the worn pipe itself. The report from a detection visit helps with a claim.
