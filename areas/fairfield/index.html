Fairfield homes by era, and what fails first

Most Fairfield houses were built in the second half of the twentieth century, a mix of slab ranches, split-levels and basement homes. That age range is right where original equipment runs out. Tank water heaters, washing machine hoses, toilet fill valves and the copper lines in the oldest sections are the parts that fail most.

The result is a very different call pattern from Hamilton's historic districts. In Fairfield it is less often galvanized pipe and lead lines, and more often a 12-year-old water heater leaking into a finished basement or a pinhole in a copper line above a drop ceiling.

Fairfield water is softened at the plant

Fairfield runs its own water system, separate from Hamilton's. The city pumps from six deep wells in the Great Miami Buried Valley Aquifer and lime-softens the water at its treatment plant on Groh Lane, beside Water Works Park. The city reports average hardness of about 128 mg/L, or 7.2 grains per gallon.

That is moderately hard, not extreme. Many Fairfield homes do fine without a softener, though tankless water heaters still benefit from a yearly flush. It also means water service line and meter questions go to Fairfield Public Utilities, not Hamilton's water department.

Slab ranches: finding a leak under the floor

Fairfield's slab ranches run their water lines under or through the concrete floor. When one of those lines develops a pinhole, the signs are subtle: a warm patch of floor above a hot water line, a damp spot at the base of a wall, or a water bill that creeps up with no change in use.

The first step is a meter test with every fixture off. If the meter moves, the leak is confirmed, and slab leak detection narrows it to a small area using pressure testing and acoustic listening.

The Ohio Plumbing Code, based on the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), requires pipe passing through concrete to be protected against corrosion, which many older slab installations lacked. That is one reason a line that leaked once is often rerouted overhead instead of patched under the slab.
